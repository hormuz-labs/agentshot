# The editor's craft

What experienced editors do without thinking, written down. SKILL.md has the rules that matter on every edit; this
file has the reasons and the detail. Read the section you need: choosing cuts (1–4), shot selection (5), pacing (6),
structures for common films (7), "cinematic" (8), colour (9), sound (10), text (11), what looks amateur (12).

## 1. What makes a cut good (Walter Murch's rule of six)
When a cut can't satisfy everything, give up the lower ones first:
1. **Emotion** (most important by far) — does the cut carry the feeling of the moment?
2. **Story** — does it move the story forward or make it clearer?
3. **Rhythm** — does it fall at an interesting, right-feeling moment (a beat, a breath, the end of a gesture)?
4. **Eye-trace** — will the viewer's eye find the subject at once in the new shot (see 3)?
5. **Screen direction / 2D plane** — does movement and looking keep its direction on screen (see 2)?
6. **3D space** — is it physically consistent (positions, props, light)?
A cut with perfect continuity but no emotion is worse than an emotional cut with a small continuity error.

## 2. Continuity: keeping the viewer oriented
- **180° rule**: between two people (or a person and what they look at) there is an imaginary line. Shots from one side
  keep A on the left looking right and B on the right looking left. Cutting to a shot from the other side flips them and
  confuses. Cross the line only through a shot that moves across it, or a neutral shot (head-on, from straight behind).
- **Screen direction of movement**: a car driving left→right should keep going left→right across cuts. A shot in which
  it moves right→left reads as coming back. To change direction, put a neutral shot between (toward or away from camera)
  or show the turn.
- **Eyelines**: when someone looks off-screen to the right, the next shot (what they see) should feel like it's to their
  right; the reverse shot of the other person looks left.
- **30° rule**: two consecutive shots of the same subject must differ by at least ~30° in angle **or** clearly in size
  (wide → medium → close). Smaller changes look like a mistake (a jump cut). In a talking head, a jump cut is either
  deliberate (fine for YouTube pacing) or hidden by a punch-in of ~115–130 % on the second part, or by a cutaway.
- **Continuity of action**: when a movement continues across a cut (opening a door, sitting down), it must continue —
  not repeat, not skip. Overlap by 0 frames; check the frame before and after the cut.

## 3. Eye-trace and motion across a cut
- At the end of shot A, the viewer is looking at a point (a face, a moving object). Put the subject of shot B near that
  point, or the eye has to search (the cut feels harsh). Moving to a new place on purpose is fine for punctuation.
- **Motion into motion**: cut moving to moving in the same direction and similar speed, or still to still. Cutting a fast
  pan into a locked-off shot is a jolt — use it only as a deliberate smash.
- **Camera moves**: let a move complete, or cut in the middle of a move into a shot moving the same way. Never cut on the
  frames where a handheld or gimbal move starts or stops (it looks like a stutter): trim those first/last 0.3–1 s.
- **Cutting on action**: cut during a movement (about a third of the way into it) — the motion hides the cut. A person
  standing up: cut as they rise, finish the rise in the next shot.

## 4. Kinds of cuts and transitions (and when)
| Cut | What it does | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Hard cut | instant change | 95 % of all cuts |
| Match cut | similar shape, motion or composition across the cut | linking places/times elegantly (a spinning wheel → a spinning coin) |
| Cut on action | movement hides the cut | any action sequence |
| Cutaway / insert | a detail or reaction, then back | hiding jump cuts, showing what's talked about |
| J cut / L cut | sound leads / trails the picture by 0.2–1 s | smooth dialogue and scene changes |
| Smash cut | abrupt loud ↔ quiet contrast | comedy, shock, a big reveal |
| Cross-cut | alternate two places happening at once | tension, parallel stories |
| Montage | many short shots compressing time | progress, travel, training, product features |
| Cross-dissolve (0.5–1.5 s) | soft blend | time passing, dreamy mood; sparingly |
| Dip to black (0.3–1 s) | full stop | end of a chapter, before a title |
| Fade from/to black | start or end | only the very start or end, and only when the mood is slow |
| Whip / flash transition | energy on a beat | rarely, on a strong beat, one style per film |
Never use: star wipes, page curls, cube spins, glitch/RGB-split, random zoom transitions — they date the film instantly.

## 5. Choosing and trimming shots
- Pick takes in this order: in focus → subject visible and well framed → steady (see `analyze_video` shake; stabilise or
  skip "very shaky" unless the shake is the point) → well exposed → good performance/moment.
- Trim the head and tail of every clip: the operator pressing record, the camera settling, the reframing at the end.
  Start when the shot is "on"; end before it drifts.
- Every shot should have a reason: it shows something new, answers a question or sets up the next shot. If you can't say
  why it's there, cut it.
- **Coverage pattern**: establish (wide: where are we?) → people (medium: who?) → detail (close: what matters?) → back
  out when the place or situation changes. Sequences that never show a wide feel claustrophobic; sequences of only wides
  feel distant.
- Variety: alternate sizes (wide/medium/close), angles (high/low/eye level), still and moving, subject and environment.
  Two consecutive shots of the same size, angle and motion look like a mistake.
- Detail shots (hands, textures, product details, faces' reactions) are what make a film feel crafted. Use them.
- Reaction shots carry emotion: in interviews and events, the listener's face is often better than the speaker's.

## 6. Pacing and rhythm
- Average shot lengths that feel right (defaults, adjust to the music and content):

| Film | Average shot | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Social short / Reel / TikTok | 1–3 s | hook in the first second; a visual change every 1–3 s |
| Commercial / promo (15–60 s) | 1–2.5 s | faster in montage, held on hero and logo |
| Trailer | 1–3 s, accelerating | holds for title cards and big lines |
| Music video | on beats/bars | follows the track's energy |
| Brand film / "cinematic" piece | 3–6 s | fewer, more beautiful shots; slow moves |
| Vlog / YouTube talking | 3–8 s per idea, jump cuts ok | b-roll every 10–20 s to illustrate |
| Interview / documentary | 4–10 s | let answers land; cutaways to hide edits |
| Tutorial | as long as the step needs | never cut away from the action being explained |

- Rhythm is variation: long–short–short–long. Uniform shot lengths feel mechanical, however "fast".
- Give important moments room: a reveal, a reaction or a punchline gets +0.5–1 s of hold before the next cut.
- Speed up toward climaxes (shorter shots), slow down after them (longer shots). That contour is the film's emotion.
- Remove dead time ruthlessly: walking to the spot, waiting, repeated explanations, the second-best take of the same
  moment. If a shot is not getting better, it's over.

## 7. Structures that work (with timings)
**Commercial / product promo (15 / 30 / 60 s)**
1. Hook (0–2 s): the most striking image (the product in its best moment, a surprising action). No logo first.
2. Desire / problem (2–8 s): the situation, the feeling, details that make you want it.
3. Reveal (on a music lift): the hero shot of the product, held 1.5–3 s.
4. Proof / features (montage on beats): 3–6 shots of it doing what it does, 1–2 s each, details and action.
5. Payoff: the emotional result (the person enjoying it, the car on the open road).
6. End card (last 2–3 s): logo/product/line, held on the final hit until the music rings out. One call to action.
For cars specifically: details first (badge, headlight, grille, wheel, stitching), then movement (low angles, tracking,
passes), then the hero (three-quarter front view, best light), interior/driver moment, ending on the car with the logo.
Low angles make cars powerful; golden light and reflections sell them; never show shaky or dull flat-light shots in the
hero slot.

**Trailer**: cold open (a striking moment, 3–8 s) → title/brand → the world (calm) → the turn (music drops out, a line)
→ escalation (accelerating montage) → title card on a hit → button (one last surprising shot or line) → end card.

**YouTube video**: hook (the result/promise in 5–15 s) → short intro → content in chapters with a "reason to keep
watching" every 60–90 s (a question, a cliffhanger, a reveal) → payoff → short outro/end screen (≤ 20 s).

**Event / travel / recap**: place (establishing wides, signage, arrival) → people (faces, interactions) → details
(food, decor, hands) → peak moment (the performance, the speech, the view) → wind-down (sunset, leaving, a last smile).
Cut to music (music-sync.md).

**Tutorial / how-to**: the result first (3–5 s) → what you need → steps in order (each: show, then do; zoom/crop into
detail when it matters) → the result again → recap.

**Interview / testimonial**: the strongest line first as a hook → context → story in the speaker's words with b-roll
over the parts that need illustration (and to hide edits) → strong closing line.

## 8. What "cinematic" means (and how to do it here)
When a user asks for "cinematic", they mean most of these — offer them as choices, don't force all:
- **Steadier camera**: stabilise shaky shots (vid.stab), keep deliberate slow moves. Shake is the fastest way to look amateur.
- **Fewer, longer, better shots**: drop the weak ones; hold on beauty; slow pushes and reveals.
- **Grade**: correct first (exposure, white balance, matching shots), then a gentle look — soft S-curve contrast, slightly
  lifted blacks, a little less saturation overall, warm skin and highlights with cooler shadows (subtle teal/orange),
  consistent across shots. Nothing should look radioactive. A soft vignette (very subtle) helps focus.
- **Aspect**: a 2.39:1 or 2:1 letterbox (crop/bars) signals cinema. Ask first; check that heads, products and text stay
  inside the frame; apply to every shot.
- **Motion**: slow motion on emotional moments only if the source is high frame rate (≥ 50/60 fps → 40–50 % speed looks
  smooth); speed ramps on beats (music-sync.md). Don't change the film's frame rate to fake "24p".
- **Sound**: music-led, with sound design (whooshes on transitions, risers into reveals, low impacts on hits, ambience
  under quiet shots); no raw wind noise or handling noise. The mix breathes: quiet moments before big ones.
- **Titles**: minimal, wide-tracked capitals or a clean serif, slow fades, centred or lower-left, plenty of space.
- **Structure**: open on an image, not on information; build to one peak; end on a held final image.

## 9. Colour basics
- Order: fix exposure → fix white balance (neutral greys, natural skin) → contrast → saturation → look. Never grade a
  look over uncorrected footage.
- Use `analyze_video`'s numbers: luma (exposure), the low–high spread (contrast), saturation and U/V offsets (colour cast).
  Bring outliers toward their neighbours so shots match; a sequence should feel like one place and time of day.
- Skin is the reference: if faces look right, most viewers accept the rest.
- Gentle is the rule: +/-10–15 % brightness or contrast, saturation 0.9–1.15, small temperature shifts. Big moves band,
  clip and look fake. Check highlights (sky, white cars, windows) don't clip after lifting exposure.

## 10. Sound fundamentals
- Order of importance: speech intelligibility → no distractions (clicks, hum, wind, level jumps) → music/sound design.
- Level-match clips: speech within about ±1 dB between shots; jumps in loudness are noticed instantly.
- Clean speech: high-pass at ~80 Hz removes rumble; reduce constant hum/noise gently (too much sounds underwater).
- Room tone: fill gaps under cuts with the location's quiet; digital silence sounds like a dropout.
- Every audio edit gets a 10–30 ms fade. Music edits on downbeats (music-sync.md).
- Natural sound (nat sound) of the footage — wheels, wind, crowd — under the music, ducked, makes montages feel real;
  bring it up for a moment of silence in the music.
- Deliver at -14 LUFS integrated, ≤ -1 dBTP for web/social; -16 LUFS for podcasts; broadcast -23/-24 LUFS if asked.

## 11. Text on screen
- Inside the safe area (≥ 5–6 % from the edges; for vertical social, out of the top ~12 % and bottom ~22 %).
- One idea per card; ≤ 6–8 words; readable twice (~0.3 s per word, minimum 1.5 s); appear and leave on beats.
- Hierarchy: one size for the main line, smaller for supporting text. Two typefaces at most; usually one.
- Contrast: light text on dark areas (or a subtle shadow/plate); never over busy detail or faces.
- Lower thirds: name on top, role below, left-aligned in the lower third, 3–6 s.

## 12. What makes an edit look amateur (avoid all of these)
- A slow start: logo, black, "hi guys", or a weak shot first.
- Shaky footage left in; shots that start/end on the camera settling; dead shots held too long.
- Cuts that ignore the music (off-beat cuts, music that doesn't end with the film, music faded mid-bar).
- Every cut the same length; or random lengths with no relation to the content.
- Many different transitions; any flashy preset transition; zooms on everything.
- Jump cuts between nearly identical shots (unless the style is deliberate and consistent).
- Mismatched colour between consecutive shots; crushed or blown-out images.
- Loudness jumps, clicks at cuts, music drowning speech, pumping ducking.
- Text too small, too fast, over faces, or with spelling mistakes.
- Black frames, frozen frames, a flash of the wrong shot at a cut.
- An ending that just stops, or drags on after the last moment.
